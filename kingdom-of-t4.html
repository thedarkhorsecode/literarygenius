A brief field report from the Kingdom of T4

No Good Deed
Goes Unpunished

A modest study of invisible heroism, institutional memory, and the strange organisational urge to celebrate a crisis only after it has been prevented.

Scroll for the report ↓

In the ancient kingdom of Vuepacs, there existed a curious guild known as T4.

The kingdom depended on T4 much in the way a human body depends on its liver: absolutely, continuously, and with very little enthusiasm to discuss it at dinner parties.

The liver, after all, is not glamorous. Nobody writes songs about a liver. No prince ever rode into battle proclaiming, “Fear not, my people, for the liver shall save us!”

Likewise, nobody ever strode into a quarterly review announcing, “Ladies and gentlemen, let us take a moment to appreciate the seventy-three obscure customer escalations prevented, the six catastrophic regressions quietly avoided, the architectural messes untangled before dawn, and the codebase which, through divine intervention and T4 stubbornness, continues functioning.”

No. Such things are considered routine.

Two kinds of work

In Vuepacs, there were two classes of work. The first involved grand adventures. One could invent something shiny. Launch a strategic initiative. Create a dashboard. Hold alignment sessions to align people on alignment. Present colourful slides filled with upward-pointing arrows and words like “Transformation” and “Vision.”

These deeds generated excitement.

The second involved ensuring the kingdom did not spontaneously combust. This was less celebrated. T4 belonged almost exclusively to the second class.

A customer disaster averted? Expected.

A critical defect resolved? Necessary.

A release saved from imminent catastrophe? Part of the job.

A decade of product knowledge applied at precisely the right moment to avoid six months of future pain? Difficult to quantify. And thus, somehow, invisible.

The curious thing was that everyone in the kingdom agreed that product knowledge was valuable. Nobody disputed this. Every speech praised ownership. Every strategy deck celebrated accountability. Everyone nodded gravely when discussing deep expertise.

“People must own the product.”
“People must understand the code.”
“People must think end-to-end.”
“People must protect the customer experience.”

These declarations were repeated with almost religious devotion. Yet whenever ownership actually appeared, wearing muddy boots and carrying twenty years of accumulated complexity on its back, people quietly stepped aside and allowed T4 to carry it.

Ownership, it turned out, was admired from a distance. Like mountain climbing. Or military service. Or taxes. Excellent things for someone else to do.

“Entire presentations were constructed to prove that bad things had not happened.”— a surprisingly difficult category of success

The evidence of nothing happening

As a result, T4 became the kingdom’s official custodian of all difficult, unpleasant, urgent, obscure, complicated, inherited, mysterious, undocumented, politically inconvenient, historically cursed, and technically hazardous work. The arrangement was widely considered efficient. For everyone except T4.

Now and then, someone would ask what value T4 provided. This question delighted the guild immensely. For it was usually posed shortly after T4 had prevented a catastrophe. A catastrophe which, by virtue of being prevented, no longer existed.

The team would then begin preparing evidence. Charts were created. Metrics were gathered. Incidents were categorized. Slides emerged. Data was assembled. Numbers were scrutinized. Hours disappeared.

One might as well attempt to quantify the value of a parachute by counting successful absences of splattering. A surgeon doesn’t receive applause for patients who didn’t die. A goalkeeper’s greatest achievements are frequently indistinguishable from nothing happening at all.

Likewise, T4’s finest work often appeared in statistics as remarkably ordinary days. And ordinary days, regrettably, never win awards.

Flowers for the forgotten plant

At one point, the guild became sufficiently puzzled by this paradox that they voiced their concerns. The kingdom reacted quickly. Appreciations were announced. Messages were circulated. Recognition appeared. Meetings were held. Gratitude was generously distributed.

The exercise lasted approximately as long as a seasonal marketing campaign.

The team, being experts in root-cause analysis, performed one immediately. Within minutes they reached a unanimous conclusion: “This is cosmetic.” Nobody wished to say it aloud. Yet everyone understood.

Recognition that appears only after people complain bears a striking resemblance to placing flowers on a plant one has forgotten to water. The flowers are appreciated. The plant remains unconvinced.

Time passed. The announcements faded. The priorities returned. The patterns repeated. And the original hypothesis gained credibility. Perhaps nobody really cared. Or perhaps they cared in exactly the same way that people care about electricity: they become aware of its importance only when it disappears.

The central mystery

Is the problem that people cannot see value? Possibly. Human beings are notoriously attracted to visible achievements. A bridge under construction attracts more admiration than one that has stood perfectly for twenty years. A new building receives attention. The foundations receive none. Yet nobody lives on the curtains. Everyone lives on the foundations.

Or perhaps something else is occurring. Perhaps there exists in every organization a fascination with the precious. The strategic. The marketable. The visible. The things that can be announced. The things that look impressive in promotion packets and quarterly reviews.

Against such things, maintenance appears humble. Expertise appears ordinary. Reliability appears boring. The paradox, of course, is that the precious survives only because the unglamorous continues doing its work. The spotlight shines because someone remembered to keep the electricity running.

And so T4 continues. It fixes. It investigates. It owns. It remembers. It carries institutional knowledge nobody else wishes to inherit. It solves problems that nobody notices because they cease to be problems.

And when the next crisis arrives, as crises always do, the kingdom will once again look around and ask: “Who understands this system?”

The answer will be exactly the same as it has always been: the guild standing quietly in the corner. The one that everyone claims is invaluable. The one perpetually asked to prove it. The one that keeps the kingdom standing while the rest debate where credit should go.

After all, in Vuepacs, as in life, no good deed goes unpunished.
